# Postmodern Architecture

**Author:** Konrad Sikorski

Postmodern architecture is a movement from roughly the 1960s through the 1980s that brought color, ornament, humor, and historical references back into buildings after decades of plain modernist glass-and-steel boxes. Postmodern architects borrowed columns, arches, and pediments from classical buildings, but used them playfully, often exaggerated, flattened, or painted in bright colors.

If modernism said a building should be a pure, honest machine, postmodernism said a building should talk to the people walking past it.

## What it is

Core characteristics:

- **Historical references:** columns, arches, gables, and pediments borrowed from classical and traditional buildings
- **Irony and humor:** familiar forms used in exaggerated, oversized, or unexpected ways
- **Color and ornament:** bright paint, patterns, and decoration, all things modernism rejected
- **"Double coding":** buildings designed to speak to two audiences at once, with references architects would recognize and friendly, familiar shapes the public would enjoy
- **Symbolism:** the outside of a building communicates what it is, like a sign, instead of only expressing its structure

## Historical context

The movement's starting point is usually traced to architect Robert Venturi. His 1966 book *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture* argued that modernism had become boring and rigid, and he answered Mies van der Rohe's famous motto "less is more" with "less is a bore." His Vanna Venturi House (1964), built for his mother in Philadelphia, put a split gable on a simple house, a direct challenge to modernist rules.

In 1972, Venturi, Denise Scott Brown, and Steven Izenour published *Learning from Las Vegas*, which argued that architects should learn from the signs and roadside buildings of ordinary commercial America instead of looking down on them.

Critic Charles Jencks popularized the term "post-modern architecture" and famously dated the "death of modern architecture" to the 1972 demolition of the Pruitt-Igoe housing project in St. Louis, a modernist project widely seen as a failure.

The style peaked in the late 1970s and 1980s, especially in corporate buildings, then faded in the 1990s as many critics came to see it as shallow.

## How it challenges modernism

| Modernism | Postmodern architecture |
|---|---|
| Less is more | Less is a bore |
| No ornament | Ornament and decoration return |
| Break with history | Borrow from history, playfully |
| Universal "international style" | Local, symbolic, communicative |
| Form expresses structure | Form expresses meaning |

## When to use it

Postmodern design ideas work well when a brand wants to feel:

- **Friendly and approachable**, not cold and corporate
- **Witty**, with a sense of humor about tradition
- **Rooted in history**, but not stuffy, like museums, theaters, and hospitality brands
- **Bold and memorable**, as a landmark or a building people talk about

It is a poor fit when a project needs to feel cutting-edge, minimal, or purely technical.

## How to apply it

**Shapes:** Use simplified classical forms like arches, columns, and pediments as flat, graphic shapes rather than realistic details.

**Color:** Pair muted pastels (salmon, teal, cream) with one or two strong accents. Many postmodern buildings used color to separate parts of a facade.

**Scale:** Exaggerate one familiar element, like an oversized keystone, a giant column, or a huge doorway.

**Symbolism:** Ask what the building or design should *say*, then make that message visible.

**In graphic and brand design:** Use arches, columns, and classical shapes as frames or logos, with a playful color palette.

## Examples

**Vanna Venturi House (Robert Venturi, 1964, Philadelphia).** A small house with a split gable on the front and an off-center chimney. It looks like a child's drawing of a house, on purpose, and it is often called the first major postmodern building.

**Piazza d'Italia (Charles Moore, 1978, New Orleans).** A public plaza made of brightly colored, neon-lit classical columns and arches, built to honor the city's Italian American community. It is theatrical and almost like a stage set.

**Portland Building (Michael Graves, 1982, Portland, Oregon).** A city office building with a cream facade, giant stylized "columns," and a huge flat keystone shape. It was celebrated and criticized in equal measure, and it became one of the symbols of the movement.

**AT&T Building, now 550 Madison (Philip Johnson, 1984, New York).** A corporate skyscraper topped with a broken pediment that reminded people of an antique Chippendale cabinet. Johnson had been a leading champion of modernism, so his switch was a major statement.

**Team Disney Building (Michael Graves, 1990, Burbank, California).** Disney's headquarters uses giant statues of the Seven Dwarfs as columns holding up the roof, a perfect example of postmodern humor and corporate branding.

## What goes wrong

Postmodern architecture can feel like a joke that gets old fast. Critics called many postmodern buildings shallow, with historical shapes slapped on as decoration without real meaning. Some buildings also had practical problems. The Portland Building, for example, was known for small windows and maintenance issues. When the style became a corporate trend, it was often copied cheaply, producing strip malls with fake columns that gave the whole movement a bad name.
