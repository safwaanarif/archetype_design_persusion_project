# New Wave Typography

**Author:** Konrad Sikorski

New Wave typography, sometimes called "Swiss Punk," is a graphic design style from the 1970s and 1980s that took the strict, orderly Swiss Style and deliberately broke its rules. Designers layered type over images, mixed weights and sizes in the same word, spaced letters unevenly, and stepped text across the page, creating work that felt energetic and experimental.

It is a good example of postmodern design *developing* from modernism instead of rejecting it completely. New Wave designers were trained in the Swiss Style, so they knew exactly which rules they were breaking.

## What it is

Core characteristics:

- **Layering:** text, images, and shapes stacked on top of each other
- **Varied type:** different weights, sizes, and letterspacing mixed together, sometimes inside one word
- **Broken grid:** elements stepped, angled, or pushed off the grid
- **Visible structure:** rules (lines), bars, and dots used as graphic elements
- **Collage and texture:** photographic and halftone textures combined with type

## Historical context

The **Swiss Style** (also called the International Typographic Style) dominated graphic design in the 1950s and 1960s. It used clean sans-serif type, strict grids, and objective layouts. It was clear and rational, and many designers felt it had become rigid and predictable.

**Wolfgang Weingart**, a German typographer who began teaching at the Basel School of Design in Switzerland in 1968, started pushing against these rules from inside the Swiss tradition itself. He experimented with letterspacing, layering, and composition, treating typography as something expressive rather than purely neutral.

Several of his students carried the style to the United States, most famously **April Greiman**, who became one of the first designers to combine New Wave ideas with early Macintosh computer design in Los Angeles. At **Cranbrook Academy of Art** in Michigan, designer Katherine McCoy led a graduate program that pushed similar experimental, layered typography in the 1970s through the 1990s.

## How it challenges modernism

| Swiss Style (modernism) | New Wave |
|---|---|
| Strict, invisible grid | Grid bent, broken, or exposed |
| Neutral, objective type | Expressive, emotional type |
| Clear hierarchy and order | Layered, complex compositions |
| Even spacing | Experimental letterspacing |
| Less is more | Rich, textured, and busy |

New Wave **develops** modernism more than it rejects it. It keeps sans-serif type and a sense of structure, but uses them in a looser, more personal way.

## When to use it

New Wave works when a brand wants to feel:

- **Creative and experimental**, like design studios, art schools, and galleries
- **Energetic and youthful**, like music, nightlife, and fashion
- **Retro-1980s**, with a design-savvy nostalgia
- **Smart but rebellious**, showing knowledge of the rules while breaking them

It is a poor fit for content that must be read quickly and clearly, like instructions, forms, and signage.

## How to apply it

**Typography:** Start with a clean sans-serif like Helvetica, Univers, or Akzidenz-Grotesk, then vary weights and sizes and play with letterspacing.

**Layout:** Set up a grid, then deliberately break it. Step text blocks diagonally, overlap elements, and let things touch the page edges.

**Graphic elements:** Add thin rules, bars, dots, and simple shapes as visual rhythm.

**Texture:** Layer halftone photos or patterns behind or over the type.

**Color:** Often limited palettes like black and white with one or two accent colors, or bright 1980s pastels.

## Examples

**Wolfgang Weingart's posters (1970s–80s).** Weingart's posters for the Basel Kunsthalle and other institutions layered film-based photographic textures, stepped type, and overlapping shapes. They became the defining examples of the style. [View on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfgang_Weingart)

**Design Quarterly #133, "Does It Make Sense?" (April Greiman, 1986).** Instead of a normal magazine issue, Greiman made a fold-out poster: a life-size digital collage of her own body combined with type and imagery, created on an early Macintosh. It showed that computers could be used for expressive, experimental design. [View on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/April_Greiman)

**Cranbrook Academy graduate work (1970s–90s).** Under Katherine McCoy, Cranbrook students produced posters and publications with dense, layered type that pushed New Wave ideas even further. [View on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cranbrook_Academy_of_Art)

**New Wave today.** Its layered type, stepped text, and exposed grids show up in music posters, fashion brands, and design-studio websites that want a retro but intelligent look.

## What goes wrong

New Wave can easily become hard to read. Too much layering, uneven spacing, and broken grids can bury the message. Imitations that break rules randomly, without understanding the grid underneath, look messy instead of intentional. It also needs a skilled hand. The style works because the original designers were masters of the Swiss Style before they broke it.
